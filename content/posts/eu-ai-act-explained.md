The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the first broad, binding law for artificial intelligence. It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. If you build or use AI and have any link to the EU market, it matters even if your company is based somewhere else. This guide explains what it covers, who it reaches, which dates apply now after the 2026 changes, and what to do first.

## The short version
- **It is law now, and it is phased in.** Some rules already apply, such as the bans and the rules for general-purpose AI models. Others start later.
- **The high-risk rules were delayed, not cancelled.** After the 2026 AI Omnibus they apply from 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for AI built into regulated products.
- **It reaches companies outside the EU** when their AI is placed on the EU market or its output is used in the EU.
- **The next hard date is 2 December 2026.** See [what changes on 2 December 2026](/guides/eu-ai-act-2-december-2026-deadline/).

## Who it applies to
Not sure whether this covers you? Try the [seven-question EU AI Act checker](/tools/eu-ai-act-checker/).

The Act reaches beyond Europe. It covers providers that place AI systems on the EU market, deployers (organisations that use AI) located in the EU, and in some cases providers and deployers outside the EU whose AI output is used in the EU. A startup in the US or Kenya selling to EU customers can be in scope.

### Provider or deployer?
Most of the duties depend on your role, so work this out first.
- **Provider:** you build an AI system, or have it built, and place it on the market or put it into service under your own name. Providers carry the heaviest duties.
- **Deployer:** you use an AI system in your own work. You have lighter duties, such as using it as instructed, human oversight and, for some uses, telling people AI is involved.
- **You can be both.** A company that builds a product on a third-party model and sells it under its own name may count as the provider of that product.

## The four risk tiers
- **Unacceptable risk:** banned outright, for example certain manipulative uses and social scoring. These bans have applied since 2 February 2025.
- **High risk:** allowed, but with strict duties. This covers AI used in areas such as hiring, education, credit scoring, essential services, law enforcement and critical infrastructure. See [Annex III: the high-risk use cases](/guides/eu-ai-act-annex-iii-high-risk-ai/).
- **Transparency risk:** systems such as chatbots and AI-generated content must be disclosed as AI.
- **Minimal risk:** most other AI, with no specific obligations beyond existing law.

Separate rules apply to general-purpose AI models, and those obligations have applied since 2 August 2025.

## The EU AI Act timeline
| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 August 2024 | The Act enters into force. |
| 2 February 2025 | Bans on prohibited practices start to apply. |
| 2 August 2025 | Rules for general-purpose AI models and the governance structure start to apply. |
| 2 August 2026 | Transparency duties under Article 50 apply. |
| 2 December 2026 | Grace period for marking AI-generated content ends for systems already on the market. New bans on nudifier and child-abuse AI start. |
| 2 December 2027 | High-risk rules for Annex III systems apply (moved from 2 August 2026). |
| 2 August 2028 | High-risk rules for AI built into regulated products in Annex I apply. |

## What changed in 2026
The EU adopted the AI Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), which entered into force on 27 July 2026. Its biggest effect is on the high-risk deadlines:

- Stand-alone high-risk systems listed in Annex III now apply from **2 December 2027** instead of 2 August 2026.
- AI built into regulated products (Annex I) now applies from **2 August 2028**.
- Transparency duties under Article 50, such as telling people they are talking to an AI, still applied from 2 August 2026. Systems already on the market before that date got until 2 December 2026 to add machine-readable marking to AI-generated content.
- Companies that decide an Annex III system is not high-risk must still register it, but with simpler information.

The Omnibus also added new bans, including on certain AI tools that generate realistic intimate imagery or child sexual abuse material, and it widened some relief for smaller companies.

Deferred does not mean cancelled. Providers of high-risk systems still need risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight and technical documentation in place.

## What high-risk providers must prepare
- A risk management system that covers the whole lifecycle
- Data governance and data quality controls
- Technical documentation (its required content is set out in [Annex IV](/guides/eu-ai-act-annex-iv-technical-documentation/))
- Automatic logging of events
- Instructions for deployers and human oversight measures
- Accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity measures
- A [conformity assessment](/guides/eu-ai-act-conformity-assessment/) and registration before going to market

## Penalties
Fines are set in Article 99 and are in three main tiers, each capped at a fixed amount or a share of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher:
- **Banned practices:** up to 35 million euros or 7%.
- **Most other breaches, including the transparency duties:** up to 15 million euros or 3%.
- **Giving incorrect or misleading information to authorities:** up to 7.5 million euros or 1%.

For small and medium-sized businesses the lower of the two figures applies. Check the current text on EUR-Lex for the figures that apply to you.

## What to do now
1. List every AI system you build or use.
2. Work out your role, provider or deployer, for each one.
3. Work out which risk tier each system falls into. Start with [Is my AI system high-risk?](/guides/is-my-ai-system-high-risk/)
4. Check your 2 December 2026 position if you offer generative AI in the EU.
5. Start keeping records and documentation now. It is far cheaper than rebuilding it later.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the EU AI Act?
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 is the first broad, binding law for artificial intelligence. It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. It sorts AI into risk tiers and sets duties for providers and deployers.

### Who does the EU AI Act apply to?
Providers that place AI systems on the EU market, deployers located in the EU, and in some cases providers and deployers outside the EU whose AI output is used in the EU. Not sure about your case? Try the [EU AI Act checker](/tools/eu-ai-act-checker/).

### When does the EU AI Act apply?
In stages. The bans on prohibited practices have applied since 2 February 2025, and the rules for general-purpose AI models since 2 August 2025. Transparency duties under Article 50 started on 2 August 2026, with a grace period to 2 December 2026 for machine-readable marking of systems already on the market. After the 2026 AI Omnibus, high-risk rules apply from 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and from 2 August 2028 for AI built into regulated products.

### Has the EU AI Act been delayed?
Partly. The 2026 AI Omnibus moved the high-risk rules from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and to 2 August 2028 for AI built into regulated products. The bans, the general-purpose AI rules and the Article 50 transparency duties were not delayed.

### What is the AI Omnibus?
Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, which entered into force on 27 July 2026. It changed several dates in the AI Act, simplified some registration duties, widened relief for smaller companies and added new bans from 2 December 2026.

### What are the penalties under the EU AI Act?
Fines for banned practices go up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Most other breaches go up to €15 million or 3%, and giving incorrect information to authorities up to €7.5 million or 1%. For small and medium-sized businesses the lower amount applies.

### What is the difference between a provider and a deployer?
A provider builds an AI system or has it built and places it on the market under its own name. A deployer uses an AI system in its own work. Providers have the heaviest duties. A business can be both.

### Does the EU AI Act apply to US, UK or Canadian companies?
It can, if your AI system is placed on the EU market or its output is used in the EU. See the [UK](/guides/uk-ai-regulation/), [US](/guides/us-ai-regulation/) and [Canada](/guides/canada-ai-regulation/) guides.
